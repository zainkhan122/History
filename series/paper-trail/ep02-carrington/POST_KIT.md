## 2 · Description (YouTube Shorts)
> On 1 September 1859 the astronomer Richard Carrington was sketching sunspots when two patches of intensely bright white light broke out on the Sun. About seventeen hours later the strongest geomagnetic storm on record hit Earth: aurora was seen as far south as Cuba, campers in the Rocky Mountains got up at night to make breakfast, and telegraph lines sparked, shocked operators and set paper on fire. Between Boston and Portland, operators disconnected their batteries and worked the line for about two hours on the current the storm induced in the wires.
>
> In July 2012 a storm at least as strong crossed Earth's orbit. About one week earlier, and it would have hit us.

## 7 · Accuracy notes (keep for comment replies)
- The Rocky Mountain News letter describes a party sleeping in the open air, not prospectors; later retellings embellish who they were.
- The operators did not get usable energy "from the aurora". The storm's fast-changing magnetic field induced currents in long grounded wires, and the aurora is the visible sign of the same storm.
- "About seventeen hours": Carrington put the storm onset "towards four hours after midnight" (Kew magnetographs); modern reconstructions give ~17.6 h Sun–Earth transit.
- Carrington did not claim the flare caused the storm. His words: "one swallow does not make a summer."
- 2012: "at least as strong" is Baker's assessment (NASA 2014). A repeat today would be serious but uneven (power grids, satellites, radio, navigation); claims that it would switch off all electronics are not supported.
- The 12 % chance per decade figure is Pete Riley's 2012 estimate (Space Weather), as reported by NASA in 2014.
